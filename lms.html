The Problem

The same interface, serving two completely different users

Before the B2B2C expansion, Reading Ocean had one user type: students. The platform was built entirely around them. When private academies joined, instructors became primary users — but the system still only knew how to be a student platform.

The core issue: The admin interface and the student interface were identical — 100% the same layout, navigation, and visual identity. Instructors were landing in a student-facing interface and trying to do administrative work. The confusion was structural, not a usability edge case.

UI Confusion

  • Admin and student landing pages looked identical
  • Over 80% of surveyed instructors reported difficulty using the service
  • Most common CS inquiry: "Why isn't this working?" — from instructors in the student interface
  • No visual or navigational distinction between roles

Tracking Gap

  • No way for instructors to track individual student progress systematically
  • Existing reports relied on simple bar graphs with no comparative data
  • 43.8% of surveyed users identified report features as their top need
  • Instructors couldn't use the platform effectively in parent consultations
Research & Key Decisions

Using the data the company already had

1

CS inquiry data as the starting point

Rather than starting with exploratory research, I began by analyzing existing CS inquiry logs. The data showed a consistent pattern: 6% of monthly inquiries were UI-related, and the vast majority came from instructors confused about which interface they were in.

This gave the problem a measurable baseline before any design work began — and made the business case for the redesign concrete.

2

Quantitative survey to scope the report feature

A two-week survey targeting academy instructors and power users surfaced that 43.8% of respondents identified the lack of a proper progress tracking report as their primary pain point. This directed where the design effort should go beyond the UI separation.

The survey also revealed that dissatisfaction with reports stemmed specifically from the lack of comparative data — not just the absence of graphs, but the inability to see one student's performance in context.

3

In-depth interviews to understand the tracking need

Following the survey, in-depth interviews were conducted with instructors who had flagged UI concerns. The Mom Test principle guided the question design — eliminating solution bias to get at real workflow friction rather than feature requests.

The interviews confirmed the survey finding and added texture: instructors needed to talk about student progress with parents. The existing reports couldn't support that conversation.

4

SWOT analysis: finding data the platform uniquely owned

An internal SWOT analysis identified that Reading Ocean possessed unusually detailed learning data — reading comprehension broken down by critical, literal, and creative skill dimensions. This data existed but wasn't being surfaced to instructors in any meaningful way.

This led to the decision to create the C.R. Rate (Careful Reading Rate) — a proprietary metric derived from the platform's exclusive data. Instead of generic scores, instructors could now see a measure that competitors couldn't replicate, directly addressing the demand for comparative performance data.

5

A/B testing the UI separation

Three rounds of A/B testing were run on the redesigned landing pages. The tests focused on whether instructors could immediately identify which interface they were in, and whether the navigational structure reduced time-to-task for administrative functions.

The results were clear. Role-specific visual identity and navigation eliminated the ambiguity that had been generating CS inquiries.

Solution

Two problems, two solutions, both measured

Solution 01 — UI Separation

Role-specific landing pages for instructors and students

The instructor and student interfaces were fully redesigned as distinct experiences — different visual identity, different navigation structure, different information hierarchy. The goal wasn't aesthetic differentiation; it was making role confusion structurally impossible.

Instructors received a landing page organized around administrative functions: class management, student oversight, and reporting. Students retained a learning-focused interface. The two no longer shared any navigational ambiguity.

CS inquiries: 6% → 0% · Positive responses: +150%
Solution 02 — Progress Tracking & C.R. Rate

Personalized reports built around data the platform uniquely owned

The redesigned progress reports replaced generic bar graphs with visualizations built around the platform's proprietary reading skill data. The C.R. Rate gave instructors a comparative metric — "My C.R. Rate" vs "Average C.R. Rate" — that directly addressed the demand for benchmarking data surfaced in the survey.

Reports became detailed enough to support parent consultations, which had been identified as a key instructor use case in the interviews. The frequency of performance sharing with parents increased more than twofold after the redesign.

Satisfaction survey information score: +28%
Reflection

What I learned, and what I'd change

What this project taught me

The most useful research in this project was data the company already had. CS logs and satisfaction surveys gave a measurable baseline that exploratory research alone couldn't have provided. Starting with existing data — and designing to move specific numbers — made the impact of the work legible in a way that's rarely possible in greenfield projects.

What I'd do differently

The C.R. Rate was a strong insight, but it was developed after the survey phase. I'd build the SWOT analysis into the research process earlier — looking at what data assets the platform owned before scoping the feature set. The proprietary metric could have been identified as a differentiator from the start, rather than emerging mid-project.
