The brief was straightforward: build a chemical supply management system. What wasn't straightforward was getting legal requirements, internal policies, three subsidiaries' workflows, three external systems, and development constraints to coexist in a single purchase flow.

The Problem

Every stakeholder had a different version of what "compliant" meant

The starting point was clear: chemical purchases within Hanwha Aerospace's ESH division needed a proper system. When three previously independent subsidiaries merged, their separate chemical management processes — each built around different internal policies and data structures — had to be unified.

Legal & Regulatory

Chemical purchase processes are legally regulated — approval stages, required documentation, and compliance checkpoints are mandated, not optional. The flow had to be built around these requirements first.

Three Subsidiaries

Each subsidiary had its own way of handling chemical purchases before the merger. Unifying these meant resolving conflicts between different internal policies, workflows, and data ownership conventions.

System Architecture

Chemical code data needed to flow across SAP, ESH, and Master Data. Which system generated it, which stored it, and which distributed it — each option had different API and security constraints.

Development Constraints

Not everything that made sense on paper was technically feasible. API integrations were restricted in places due to security policies, requiring the flow to be redesigned around what could actually be built.

Design Process

Building the flow from the constraints outward

1

Regulatory analysis before any design work

Before touching IA or flows, I mapped the legal requirements governing chemical purchases. Approval stages, mandatory documentation fields (CAS numbers, MSDS attachments), and compliance checkpoints were non-negotiable — they defined the skeleton of the flow before any UX decisions were made.

Weekly card sorting sessions ran alongside this regulatory analysis to surface how each of the three subsidiaries actually handled purchases on the ground. The gap between what the law required and what people were actually doing became one of the central design tensions.

2

Interface flow analysis: where does the data actually live?

Chemical code data was used across SAP, ESH, and Master Data simultaneously. Deciding where it was generated and how it moved between systems wasn't a technical decision — it was a design decision with technical implications.

Multiple scenarios were evaluated: generate in SAP and transfer via API, or generate in ESH and distribute outward. Each option hit different constraints — API restrictions, security policies, data ownership rules. The final architecture was the one that could actually be built, not the cleanest one on paper.

3

Iterating the process flow until all three subsidiaries could agree

The process flow went through multiple iterations — not because the design was wrong, but because each iteration surfaced new conflicts between subsidiary workflows, legal requirements, and system constraints that couldn't be seen until the previous version was tested against them.

This was the most time-intensive part of the project. Each round of revision involved coordinating across legal, development, field teams, and system owners simultaneously.

4

Sign-Off: structuring the approval layer

The approval process for chemical purchases involved multiple departments — Health, Safety, and Environment — each with their own sign-off requirement. These had been handled separately and inconsistently across the three subsidiaries.

Mapping the full purchase flow made the fragmentation visible. The solution was a sequential Sign-Off structure: each department signs in order, with an automated alert triggering the next signatory immediately upon completion. This reduced waiting time between approvals and prevented steps from being missed.

Reflection

What I learned, and what I'd change

What this project taught me

In highly regulated enterprise environments, the design work is mostly negotiation — between what the law requires, what the organization wants, and what can actually be built. The UX output is almost secondary to the process of getting all those constraints into the same room and finding where they can coexist. Flow design here was as much about alignment as it was about usability.

What I'd do differently

I'd map the system architecture constraints earlier — ideally before starting the process flow. Discovering API and security limitations mid-iteration forced redesigns that could have been avoided. Getting developers and system owners into the same conversation as legal and field teams from the start would have compressed the iteration cycle significantly.
